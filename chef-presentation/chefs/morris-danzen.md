## Biography

From *Conversation with a Chef* #344, interviewed at the Boodle Brunch during the Melbourne Food
& Wine Festival.

**The beginning.** His father went blind when Morris was **twelve or thirteen**. He and his
younger brother took over the care, and he learned to cook — over wood, with whatever produce
there was. In his words: they were so poor they ate once a day, and as a child that was simply
normal. He credits that with training him before he knew he was being trained — limited
ingredients, no choice, cook or don't eat.

**The detour.** His first love was fine art; he drew and painted, but studying it in the
Philippines was beyond reach, so he took multimedia technology and worked as a freelance
photographer, videographer **and singer**. He went to China to work with his sister, planning to
save up and open a photography shop back home.

**Italy.** He met his wife, who is Italian, and they moved to Sicily. Photography and music work
was scarce; his in-laws ran a trattoria, so he went into the kitchen — and turned it into
Filipino-Italian fusion in a Sicilian town of 44,000 people used to eating traditional Sicilian
food. **At first, people hated it.** He brought in sweet potato, coconut milk and calamansi,
driving an hour each way to find them, and cooked them with Italian technique.

**The turn.** Pushed by his mother-in-law — "my number one fan" — he entered a televised cooking
competition in Italy **and won it, against Gino D'Acampo**. He was to become head chef in
D'Acampo's restaurant; **Brexit and the pandemic ended that**. The win still built the
restaurant's name, and people came from across Italy and America. Then the pandemic closed it for
good.

**Melbourne.** Fabio Magliano of **Buono** called about a working-holiday visa. He took it. He now
cooks **modern Italian at Buono in Parkdale**, and runs **CMD Supper Club** as his own outlet —
one Monday or Thursday night a month, in borrowed rooms, inviting chefs from abroad.

**Nearly fifteen years in.** He says he never dreamed of being a chef, and doesn't dream of a
restaurant either: he wants to travel, cook, learn from other chefs and collaborate — a bigger
radius reaches more people than a room does. He describes composing a dish the way he composed a
painting or a cover of a song: dynamics, contrast, fine lines and hard lines.

## On the boodle

He is the right person to explain the word the festival already uses.

- There is **no occasion for it.** If everyone is free on an afternoon with nothing to do, they
  pick banana leaves, cook, tip it out and eat together. It is the norm, not an event.
- **"No one eats alone in the Philippines."** A family of six will wait for the sixth. Eating
  alone is close to shameful.
- Usually done **under a tree or near the beach.**
- The Melbourne menu was **95% Filipino** with Australian produce carrying the rest — wattleseed
  lamb in the lumpia in place of pork, prawns basted in lemon myrtle oil — deliberately, because
  Filipino food is not yet widely known there.
- On why the cuisine is what it is: thousands of islands, each with its own produce and cooking;
  Malay influence in the south, more Spanish braising in the north, seafood and ceviche in the
  islands. **Adobo began as a preserving technique** — vinegar, garlic, pepper, bay — and only
  became a soy-sauce dish after the Chinese arrived. Every family has its own recipe.
